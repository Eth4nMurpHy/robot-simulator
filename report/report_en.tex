\title{\textbf{Joint Robot Scheduling with Intersection Reservations and Charging}}
\author{EE6228 Course Project}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Four robots operating on two fixed directed loops must complete pickup-and-delivery tasks
while sharing one intersection and limited charging bays. Task order, resource queues and battery
state are coupled because waiting also consumes energy. This project jointly determines robot
assignment, visit order, intersection slots, charging-bay selection and charging amount in a finite
integer scheduling model. Joint Greedy supplies an initial schedule, CP-SAT searches for improvements,
and a rolling version replans using only released tasks. Returned action traces are checked for
precedence, resource capacity and battery safety. In archived 40-task experiments over ten seeds,
CP-SAT reduces the mean composite objective $J$ by 53.2\pct\ and makespan by 28.3\pct\ relative to
FCFS (FCFS revision 75afc09; CP-SAT revision c87fb9f; eight workers and a 120\,s CP-SAT limit). For a
separate 12-task instance with seed 6, CP-SAT reduces makespan by 30.4\pct\ under one worker, a 30\,s
wall cap and a 0.5 deterministic budget. Short-budget batch runs find no improvement beyond Joint
Greedy. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of integrated task, intersection and charging
scheduling to improve performance in fixed-route multi-robot systems.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} multi-robot scheduling; charging scheduling; constraint
programming; intersection reservation; rolling horizon; discrete-event simulation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Research Background}

Multi-robot systems are widely used for transportation and material handling in automated
manufacturing and warehouse environments. Their scheduling begins with task allocation: deciding which
robot performs each task and how the robots coordinate their work. Athira et al.~\cite{athira2025mrta}
review the resulting problem classes and how coordination and uncertainty are handled.

In automated guided vehicle (AGV) systems, allocation interacts with shared traffic resources.
Li, Fan and Jia~\cite{li2024conflict} combine dynamic task assignment with conflict-free path planning
in an automated container terminal, where vehicles may not occupy the same part of the network at the
same time. A schedule that ignores such conflicts can become infeasible once vehicles meet.

Batteries add a second coupling. When chargers are scarce, charging competes with transport for both
vehicle time and charger capacity; Li et al.~\cite{li2025chargers} therefore schedule AGV tasks
jointly with charging under a limited number of chargers. Building on these lines of work, this
project investigates the joint scheduling of task execution, intersection reservations and charging
decisions in a fixed-route multi-robot system. Methods for the individual model components are
discussed where those components are introduced (Sections~\ref{sec:model} and~\ref{sec:methods}).

\subsection{System Overview and Problem Statement}

This project considers a multi-robot pickup-and-delivery system with four robots operating
on two fixed directed loops. Two robots serve each loop, and each transport task requires a
robot to collect and deliver an item between two stations on the same loop. The two loops share a
single intersection, which admits only one robot at a time. The robots also share a charging
area with up to two charging bays, each serving one robot at a time.

The scheduling problem is to determine which robot performs each task, the task execution order,
when robots traverse the shared intersection, and when and how much they charge. These
decisions must satisfy task precedence, resource capacity and battery safety constraints. The objective
is to minimise a composite measure of makespan, priority-weighted tardiness, resource waiting time and
energy consumption.

\subsection{Motivation and Scheduling Challenges}

Although each robot stays on its assigned loop for transport, all four share intersection X and the
charging area. Task assignment determines when a robot reaches its pickup station, intersection X and a
charging bay. Queues at shared resources then delay completion and consume battery energy. Makespan,
tardiness, waiting and energy are therefore linked by the same assignment and timing decisions.

For example, when a robot (say R1) receives its next task, its battery may appear sufficient to
complete the pickup and delivery yet still be insufficient to reach a charging bay safely afterwards.
The scheduler must then arrange charging first. To reach the charging area, the robot must reserve and
traverse the shared intersection. It may then need to wait for an available charging bay, consuming
additional energy while queueing. After charging, the robot must reserve the intersection again to
return to its loop. These extra movements and waiting periods may delay subsequent tasks and cause
them to miss their deadlines. Task scheduling, resource reservations and battery state must therefore
be considered jointly.

\subsection{Research Questions}

Based on these couplings, this project investigates the following questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Under identical tasks, maps, initial state-of-charge (SOC) values and offline
  information, how does joint decision-making affect makespan, total tardiness, resource waiting,
  energy consumption and the normalised composite objective $J$ compared with First-Come,
  First-Served (FCFS) and staged methods?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} What is the performance cost of requiring robots to retain enough energy at
  the end of the horizon to reach the charging area safely? How do task load, the number of charging
  bays and the distribution of tasks between Loops A and B shift the system bottleneck?
\item \textbf{RQ3:} When tasks become visible only after their release time, what scheduling
  performance can the rolling-horizon method achieve, and how much replanning and solution overhead
  does it incur?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Contributions and Report Organisation}

This project provides a joint scheduling model for task assignment, robot visit order, intersection
reservations, charging-bay selection, charging duration and SOC evolution. Offline CP-SAT and rolling
optimisation use the same discrete-event execution engine and output validator as the heuristic
baselines. Every run produces its task inputs, actions, resource reservations, SOC traces and solver
records, so aggregate results can be traced to individual decisions; the archived batch retains per-run
metrics and solver records. The main contribution is this integrated framework and its evaluation on a
fixed two-loop system.

Section~\ref{sec:problem} defines the system, its inputs and its scope.
Sections~\ref{sec:model}--\ref{sec:impl} present the model, the solution methods and the
implementation checks. Sections~\ref{sec:setup}--\ref{sec:conclusion} describe the experiments,
analyse the results, discuss limitations and conclude. The appendices give the full model, the worked
experiment's task list and schedule records, and key code excerpts.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Network and Resource Configuration}

The system contains two unidirectional loops with fixed connectivity (Figure~\ref{fig:network}).
Loop A follows X $\to$ A1 $\to$ A2 $\to$ A3 $\to$ A4 $\to$ WA $\to$ X and Loop B follows
X $\to$ B1 $\to$ B2 $\to$ B3 $\to$ B4 $\to$ WB $\to$ X. Robots R1 and R2 serve only Loop A, and R3 and R4 serve only
Loop B. WA and WB are the waiting points in front of the shared intersection X. The charging branch is
X $\to$ H $\to$ C1/C2 $\to$ H $\to$ X, where H is the charging buffer and queueing location and C1 and C2 are
configurable charging bays.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{network.png}
\caption{Network topology: two directed loops, the shared intersection X with waiting points WA and
WB, and the charging branch through H to bays C1 and C2.}
\label{fig:network}

The map has 18 directed edges. Travel time equals edge length divided by speed, and movement energy
equals edge length times a per-metre consumption rate; the map editor changes these parameters but
never the connectivity. Only X and the charging bays are exclusive resources. Several robots may use an
ordinary loop edge at once, so the model does not address geometric collision avoidance.

\code{pickup} and \code{dropoff}, two distinct stations on the same loop; \code{load\_time} and
\code{unload\_time}; \code{due\_time}; and an integer \code{priority} from 1 to 5.

\subsection{Scope and Assumptions}
\label{sec:scope}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fleet and network.} Four robots (two per loop), one shared intersection and up to two
  charging bays; each task is a single-loop pickup-and-delivery order, and a robot carries one task
  at a time. The experiments use 20--60 tasks.
\item \textbf{Exclusive resources.} X and each enabled bay hold one robot at a time, with a safety
  buffer between consecutive crossings. Robots queue for X or a bay only at WA, WB or H and never stop
  inside X or queue inside an occupied bay.
\item \textbf{Time and energy.} Durations are deterministic. SOC changes with movement, handling,
  queueing and linear charging; powered-down standby between tasks is free. Tasks, crossings and
  charging visits are non-preemptive, and a robot leaves its bay as soon as charging ends. The
  heuristics charge to 95\pct, whereas CP-SAT chooses the charging amount.
\item \textbf{Information.} The heuristics and offline CP-SAT see the whole task table but never start
  a task before its release; rolling CP-SAT sees only released tasks and keeps committed actions
  fixed. Manual command replay only executes and checks external plans and is excluded from the
  algorithm comparison.
\item \textbf{Out of scope.} Nonlinear charging, multiple intersections, a variable fleet, continuous
  kinematics and obstacle avoidance. Robots need not return to an origin; the terminal reserve only
  keeps a charger reachable after the last task.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Assignment and Temporal Constraints}

Each robot orders its assigned tasks and optional charging visits with \code{AddCircuit} through the
dummy initial vertex. A task vertex is active exactly when the task is assigned; a charging vertex is
active when the visit is enabled. Absent vertices use self-loops, and a dummy self-loop permits an
unused robot. Assignment, arc, activation and bay variables are binary. Selected arcs impose
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k \in K_i} x_{ik} = 1, \qquad s_i \ge r_i, \qquad
a_{kab} = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; s_{kb} \ge f_{ka} \quad (b \ne 0).
\label{eq:assign}
\end{equation}
Here $s_i$ is the task start (including empty travel to the pickup), $c_i$ is the unloading completion
and $f_{ka}$ is the finish time of the preceding visit. For an assigned robot $k$, $c_i$ satisfies
Eq.~\eqref{eq:completion}: the selected predecessor determines the empty-travel time, loaded travel
follows the fixed pickup-to-delivery route, and the sum adds the permitted resource queues over the set
$\mathcal{R}_{ki}$ of crossings on the selected empty and loaded paths. The closing dummy arc ends the
sequence without return travel, so the makespan equals the final delivery time.
\begin{equation}
c_i = s_i + \tau^{\text{empty}}_{ki} + L_i + \tau^{\text{loaded}}_{i} + U_i
  + \sum_{r \in \mathcal{R}_{ki}} w_{kr}.
\label{eq:completion}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Resource Capacity and Battery Safety}

Every selected crossing reserves the interval $[e_{kr},\, l_{kr} + \delta)$ in one \code{NoOverlap}
constraint, where $e_{kr}$ and $l_{kr}$ are the entry and exit times and $\delta = 2\beta$; the default
buffer parameter of 2\,s therefore gives a 4\,s clearance between crossings. This models the
intersection as a time-reserved resource, following reservation-based autonomous intersection
management~\cite{dresner2008}. Robots wait at WA, WB or H and may not stop inside X.

Each selected charging visit queues at H, enters one chosen bay, charges without interruption and
immediately returns to H; bay intervals are also non-overlapping. Bay selection satisfies
$\sum_{p} b_{kqp} = y_{kq}$. With arrival time $h$ at H, charging begins at
$u = h + w^{C} + \tau_{Hp}$ and the visit ends at $u + D + \tau_{pH}$. Charging amount and duration are
decision variables, as in AGV scheduling with flexible charging under a limited number of
chargers~\cite{li2025chargers} and variable charging durations~\cite{li2025battery};
the heuristics instead use a fixed 95\pct\ target.

SOC decreases with movement, handling and resource queueing, while powered-down standby between tasks
is free. For a segment without replenishment and with $n_h$ handling operations,
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{out}} = S_{\text{in}} - \sum_{(u,v)} g_{uv} - n_h\, g_h - \rho_w \sum w ,
\label{eq:soc}
\end{equation}
which is checked before replenishment and after each action, within $[S_{\min}, 100]$. For a charging visit
with SOC $S^{H}$ on arrival at H, queue $w^{C}$ and charging duration $D$, safe arrival, replenishment
and departure require
\begin{equation}
S^{-} = S^{H} - \rho_w w^{C} - g_{Hp} \ge S_{\min}, \qquad
S^{+} = S^{-} + \rho_c D \le 100, \qquad
S^{+} - g_{pH} \ge S_{\min}.
\label{eq:charge}
\end{equation}

The default terminal reserve requires every robot that serves tasks to keep enough energy after its
final delivery to reach a bay and withstand conservative queueing, including an allowance for three
other recharges; the integer model adds a 0.5-percentage-point margin, and the decoded plan is checked
against the actual calendars. The \code{cpsat-nores} ablation removes only this terminal requirement
and keeps battery safety during execution; it is evaluated separately because it permits more
schedules. Rolling optimisation always keeps the reserve.

\subsection{Objective and Model Scope}

The optimiser minimises a composite objective of makespan, tardiness, waiting and energy. With
$T_i = \max(0,\, c_i - d_i)$, $C_{\max} = \max_i c_i$, total intersection and charging queues $W_X$
and $W_C$, and total positive consumption $E$,
\begin{equation}
\min\ J = \frac{1}{4}\left( \frac{C_{\max}}{\hat R_C} + \frac{\sum_i p_i T_i}{\hat R_T}
  + \frac{W_X + W_C}{\hat R_W} + \frac{E}{\hat R_E} \right).
\label{eq:J}
\end{equation}
Combining energy and delay follows multi-objective scheduling of multi-load AGVs that
balances handling delays against energy consumption~\cite{huo2024multiload}. The references normally
come from FCFS on the same instance, with denominator floors of 1\,s, 60\,s, 60\,s and 1 SOC percentage
point. If FCFS fails, Staged and Deadline are tried before floor-only references; with these fallbacks and floors,
FCFS need not score exactly 1. Energy excludes replenishment, and weighted tardiness differs from the
unweighted CSV field \code{total\_tardiness}. Battery safety is a hard constraint, not a compensable
objective term.

CP-SAT uses a 1\,ms time grid, conservative integer approximations and a finite number of optional
charging slots. OPTIMAL refers to this bounded integer model, and every candidate is decoded and
validated before it is returned.

\section{Solution Methodology}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Baselines and Joint Greedy Scheduling}

All automatic methods share the executor, map, energy rules and metrics. Table~\ref{tab:policies}
summarises their decision rules. FCFS charges before a task when SOC is below 20\pct; accepted tasks
must also pass completion and terminal-reserve checks.

\centering\small

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{pipeline.png}
\caption{Joint planning and validation pipeline: solver, decoder, validator and incumbent fallback.
The same execution rules apply to all automatic strategies.}
\label{fig:pipeline}

Joint Greedy considers up to eight pending tasks in release-time order and both compatible robots.
It evaluates each candidate on a cloned state by attempting the task, checking its post-task reserve and
inserting charging first if needed. Infeasible candidates are discarded without changing the committed
schedule. The selected candidate minimises the local score
\begin{equation}
\text{score}_{ik} = c_i + 3\, p_i T_i + 0.5\, \Delta W_i + 2\, \Delta E_i ,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
with task and robot identifiers breaking ties; the incremental wait $\Delta W_i$ and positive
consumption $\Delta E_i$ include any inserted charging visit. The score uses raw units and fixed
coefficients rather than the normalised $J$. It gives a fast joint initial schedule. The heuristics see
the full task list but never start a task before its release. If no candidate passes the checks, the
strategy reports that no safe plan was found. Learning-based dispatchers, such as the hierarchical reinforcement-learning scheduler of Chang
et al.~\cite{chang2025rl}, offer an alternative, but explicit rules are kept here so that every decision
can be checked against the constraints.

\subsection{Offline Joint Optimisation}

Integrated machine--AGV scheduling has been formulated as a mixed-integer program~\cite{yao2025milp}
and as a constraint program that explicitly handles whether transport tasks
exist~\cite{yao2026cp}. This project likewise activates a visit only when it is selected, and solves
the model with the CP-SAT solver of Google OR-Tools~\cite{cpsat}. CP-SAT combines lazy clause
generation with linear relaxations and a portfolio of parallel search workers, including large
neighbourhood search~\cite{perron2023cpsat}. It fits this problem because assignment and
visit choices are Boolean, while optional intervals, \code{AddCircuit} and \code{NoOverlap} represent
sequence and resource capacity directly; linear time and SOC constraints connect those choices to
feasible execution. Joint Greedy supplies an initial physical schedule and, when it can be projected
onto the grid, an \code{AddHint} search hint that guides the search without fixing decisions. The valid
initial schedule is also retained outside the solver.

The simulator's planner decodes the selected circuits using the model's visit order, crossing times,
bay choices and charging targets. It recomputes actual durations and SOC changes from the map instead of
copying integer variables, and checks both the resulting actions and the enabled terminal reserve. The
returned plan is the valid decoded candidate or the admissible heuristic incumbent, whichever has the
lower actual $J$, so CP-SAT never returns a worse plan than Joint Greedy.

Every run stores the solver status, bound, gap, decoded objective and fallback reason, separately from
the validation result of the returned schedule. Overall runtime includes initial planning, model
construction, solving, decoding and validation, whereas solver wall time covers search only.

\subsection{Rolling Horizon Optimisation}

At decision time $t$, rolling optimisation sees only released, uncommitted tasks. As in rolling-horizon
allocation of dynamically arriving warehouse AGV tasks~\cite{xin2024dynamic}, the plan is recomputed as
information arrives: by default every 60\,s, over a window of roughly 600\,s of workload capacity per
robot. Tasks are prioritised by due time, priority and identifier. The window limits the work
considered rather than imposing a completion deadline: travel to pickups, queueing or charging can
extend the plan beyond it. Current robot states and committed reservations define the next solve's
initial conditions. Receding-horizon re-optimisation has also been used to reorder AGVs through shared
intersections during execution~\cite{berndt2024receding}, much as the rolling variant here
revises crossing and charging slots.

At each epoch, the planner commits only the prefix of each robot's visits that starts before the next
decision epoch. The whole committed visit is frozen, including actions and reservations that extend
beyond that epoch; ongoing tasks and charging are not interrupted, while uncommitted tasks may be
reassigned later. Each solve normalises with the currently known tasks and state; full-instance
references are used only for the retrospective score, so unreleased orders affect neither current
decisions nor objective coefficients.

\section{System Implementation and Validation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Shared Execution and Traceability}

LoopLab combines a Python backend with browser replay. \code{simulator.py} maintains robot states,
reservation calendars, action generation, metrics and validation; \code{optimizer.py} provides joint
and rolling optimisation; \code{experiments.py} reuses instances and aggregates results. The front end
visualises the returned trace rather than running another scheduler. Each action records robot and task
identifiers, endpoints, start and end times, SOC, consumption and resources. Events, Gantt charts,
battery curves and CSV/JSON exports all refer to the same run, so any aggregate result can be traced to
specific actions and reservations.

Strict command replay provides a separate interface for \code{execute\_task}, \code{move\_to},
\code{charge} and \code{wait} commands with absolute start times. It rejects timing, resource and
battery conflicts instead of queueing commands or inserting charging automatically; charging robots
remain parked until explicitly moved, and omitted tasks remain unexecuted. Manual plans are therefore
kept separate from the automatic algorithm ranking.

\subsection{Independent Validation}

The validator recomputes correctness from the output tasks, actions, map and reservations. It checks
unique task execution, release and pickup-before-delivery precedence, robot continuity and
non-overlap, legal directed travel, load capacity, X and bay occupancy and clearance, legal waiting
locations, and SOC continuity and bounds. Rebuilding occupancy from actions also detects missing or
inconsistent reservation records. A candidate that fails these checks is rejected, even if the
optimiser reports feasibility.

A one-task check uses A1 to A2, release 0, due time 600\,s, priority 1, 15\,s loading and unloading,
and initial SOC 80\pct. The default 20\,m edge takes 20\,s and consumes 2 SOC points, and each handling
operation consumes 0.5, so completion is 50\,s and final SOC is 77\pct. With the FCFS references and
floors, $J = \tfrac14\left(\tfrac{50}{50} + \tfrac{0}{60} + \tfrac{0}{60} + \tfrac{3}{3}\right) = 0.5$.
Table~\ref{tab:handcheck} compares this calculation with a fresh program run. A forged final SOC of
78\pct\ fails the consistency check even though it exceeds the safety floor.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Evaluation Questions and Dataset Generation}

RQ1 evaluates whether joint scheduling improves completion time, tardiness, waiting and energy
relative to staged and single-rule baselines; RQ2 examines terminal reserves and resource sensitivity;
RQ3 examines online solution quality and replanning cost. The experiments use synthetic tasks for the
four-robot, dual-loop system.

For $n$ base tasks, releases are sampled uniformly from a 10\,s grid within $[0, 12n)$ seconds.
Pickup and delivery stations are distinct and belong to the same loop. Loading and unloading each take
10, 15 or 20\,s, and due times add 480--900\,s to the release. Priorities 1, 2 and 3 have probabilities
50\pct, 25\pct\ and 25\pct. Separate seeded streams generate urgent orders and per-robot initial SOC of
50--100\pct, rounded to one decimal, so adding urgent orders preserves the base workload.

The synthetic inputs represent recurrent transport decisions. Same-loop orders respect the fixed guideway layout; release times create competing requests; handling
durations and due allowances vary workload and urgency; and different initial SOC values create charging
demand.

\subsection{Experimental Factors and Computing Environment}

The base setting uses 40 tasks, two bays, a 10\pct\ safety threshold, equal zone-assignment
probabilities and no urgent orders. One factor varies at a time (Table~\ref{tab:factors}). Each
configuration uses seeds 1--10 and identical inputs across strategies. Ten distinct configurations
$\times$ ten seeds $\times$ seven strategies yield 700 deduplicated strategy-run records; the CSV's
1\,050 rows include repeated base results across experiment groups.

\subsection{Metrics, Statistics and Fair Comparison}

Metrics include $J$, makespan, unweighted and priority-weighted tardiness, late-task rate, resource
waiting, energy, charging sessions, runtime and violations. Average resource waiting is
$(W_X + W_C)/|I|$, excluding powered-down standby, and energy sums positive SOC consumption without
replenishment. Tables report means and sample standard deviations where shown; local figures show
medians and individual seeds. The paired objective reduction is $1 - J_s / J_{\text{FCFS}}$, so a
positive value indicates improvement; it uses the actual FCFS $J$, which can be below 1 because of the
denominator floors.

Heuristics and offline CP-SAT know future tasks but respect release times, whereas rolling CP-SAT sees
only released tasks. The main comparisons keep terminal reserves; no-reserve CP-SAT is an ablation.
All 700 archived records report success and zero violations.

\subsection{Terminal Reserve and Resource Sensitivity}

In the archived base setting, dropping the terminal reserve lowers mean $J$ from 0.468 to 0.349 and
makespan from 2\,317 to 1\,732\,s. Battery safety during execution remains enforced; only the post-batch
reserve is removed. The gain comes from a larger feasible region, at the cost that robots may end the
batch unable to reach a charger.

availability, zone imbalance tests where work is concentrated, and the safety SOC reduces the usable
battery range.

\subsection{Online Replanning}

On the local 12-task input, rolling CP-SAT performs 18 replans with no incumbent fallbacks. Total
runtime is 5.40\,s, of which solver wall time accounts for 5.10\,s; makespan is 1\,237.6\,s and $J$ is
0.505. It finishes later than offline CP-SAT but receives tasks only after their release. The archived
base mean $J$ of 0.636 also lies between FCFS and offline CP-SAT. Rolling CP-SAT therefore keeps most of
the offline gain while using only released tasks, at about 0.3\,s per replan.

%==================================================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

The archived and local experiments support the benefit of jointly scheduling task assignment,
intersection reservations and charging decisions in the tested system. Joint Greedy reduces the mean
composite objective by 20.7\pct\ relative to FCFS and provides a feasible schedule through coordinated
resource and battery checks. Offline CP-SAT achieves a further improvement under the archived search
budget, helped by its flexible charging amounts.

The worked trace identifies a concrete bottleneck: charging to a fixed 95\pct\ target can occupy a bay
long enough to delay other robots. Partial charging and task reordering remove such delays.

The short-budget results also show the importance of computational limits: CP-SAT may return the
validated Joint Greedy schedule without improving it. Joint Greedy therefore provides a practical
initial solution and fallback, while CP-SAT can improve schedule quality when sufficient search time
is available.

\subsection{Limitations}

The study covers the four-robot, fixed-route system with synthetic workloads. Validation checks the
modelled resource and battery constraints but not motion-level collision avoidance, which would need
conflict models such as that of Li, Fan and Jia~\cite{li2024conflict}. Battery behaviour is
deterministic and linear; energy uncertainty could be handled with stochastic models such as the
chance-constrained battery management developed by Ricard et al.~\cite{ricard2026chance} for electric
buses. Larger fleets, more seeds and a separate test of partial charging are natural next steps.

%==================================================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project implements joint task, intersection and charging scheduling for a fixed dual-loop system.
In the archived base study, reserve-preserving CP-SAT reduces mean $J$ by 53.2\pct\ and makespan by
28.3\pct\ relative to FCFS. The local case shows that the shorter schedule comes from removing bay queueing, and the short-budget
check shows that CP-SAT needs enough search time to improve on Joint Greedy.

The terminal reserve trades some batch performance for the guarantee that every robot can still reach
a charger after its last task. Rolling planning handles released tasks with frozen commitments and keeps
most of the offline gain. Future work could extend the model to larger fleets, uncertain energy use and
real deployment.

\section{Full Model}
\label{app:model}

The implementation uses $V_k = \{0\} \cup I_k \cup Q_k$, where $I_k$ is the set of tasks on robot $k$'s
loop and $Q_k$ its optional charging slots. Presence is $z_{ki} = x_{ik}$ for a task,
$z_{kq} = y_{kq}$ for a charging visit and $z_{k0} = 1$; assignment $x$, arc $a$, activation $y$ and
bay $b$ are Boolean. The equations below use seconds and SOC percentage points; internally, time
variables are integer ticks in $[0, \bar H]$ and node SOC and charging targets lie in
$[U S_{\min}, 100U]$. The
dummy state fixes the initial availability, position and SOC.
\begin{equation}
\sum_{k:\, i \in I_k} x_{ik} = 1, \qquad a_{knn} = 1 - z_{kn} \quad (n \ne 0), \qquad
\textsc{Circuit}(a_k).
\end{equation}
Each selected non-self arc belongs to the single circuit through 0, and the dummy self-loop permits an
idle robot. A selected arc $a \to n$ with $n \ne 0$ enforces $s_{kn} \ge f_{ka}$ and replays the fixed incoming travel
to the pickup or to H. Task copies share $s_i, c_i$ when selected, with $s_i \ge r_i$; loading, loaded
travel and unloading advance time by equality. Absent visits have zero times, and inactive crossings
and charging waits are zero. Charging visits are used in order, $y_{k,q+1} \le y_{kq}$ and
$s_{k,q+1} \ge f_{kq}$ when $y_{k,q+1} = 1$. Final charge-to-dummy arcs are forbidden, and closing task-to-dummy arcs add no
return travel.
\begin{gather}
e = \text{arrival} + w, \qquad l = e + \tau^{X}_{kr}, \qquad \textsc{NoOverlap}\{[e,\, l + \delta)\}, \\
\sum_{p} b_{kqp} = y_{kq}, \qquad u = h + w^{C} + \tau_{Hp}, \qquad f = u + D + \tau_{pH}, \\
S^{-} = S^{H} - \rho_w w^{C} - g_{Hp} \ge S_{\min}, \qquad S^{+} = S^{-} + \rho_c D \le 100, \qquad
S^{+} - g_{pH} \ge S_{\min}.
\end{gather}
Here $\tau^{X}_{kr}$ is the traversal time of the edge entering X on that crossing. Resource queueing
occurs at WA, WB or H, never inside X. The charging equalities apply only to the selected bay,
whose optional interval shares that bay's \code{NoOverlap}. In ticks, an active $D$ lies between 1 and
$\lceil 100U/\alpha \rceil$, with $\alpha = \lfloor \rho_c U / T \rfloor$. On every selected path
without charging, $S_{\text{out}} = S_{\text{in}} - \sum g - \rho_w \sum w$, including handling.
Frozen reservations are mandatory. A last delivery keeps the travel-to-bay consumption plus
$3(95 - S_{\min})\rho_w/\rho_c$ and a 0.5-point margin above the floor; decoding also checks the actual
reachability.
\begin{equation}
\min\ J = \frac{1}{4}\left( \frac{C_{\max}}{\hat R_C} + \frac{\sum_i p_i T_i}{\hat R_T}
  + \frac{W_X + W_C}{\hat R_W} + \frac{E}{\hat R_E} \right), \qquad C_{\max} = \max_i c_i .
\end{equation}

The normalisation denominators are the FCFS terms floored at $(1, 60, 60, 1)$; $E$ excludes
replenishment. Rolling solves keep fixed past contributions and normalise on known tasks only. With
$T = 1000$ ticks/s and $U = 60\,000\,000$ SOC units per point, durations, releases, consumption and
drain round up, while due dates, initial SOC and charging gain round down. Paths that are not on the
grid add 20 ticks of drain per crossing or charging approach as a model guard; this is included in the
modelled $E$ but absent from decoded actions. The visit-time bound is
$\bar H = \lceil T\,[\,t_0 + (|I| + 8)(20\,\tau_{\max} + 5000)\,] \rceil$, with $t_0$ the latest
initial or release time and $\tau_{\max}$ the longest edge travel time. Robot $k$ receives $\max(q_k^{\text{hint}},\, 1,\, \lfloor |I_k|/5 \rfloor)$
charging slots, where $q_k^{\text{hint}}$ is the number of charging visits of robot $k$ in the Joint
Greedy hint; an explicit cap also limits the fallback. The valid decoded candidate or the
admissible Joint Greedy plan with the lower actual $J$ is returned; if neither is available the run
fails. OPTIMAL refers to this bounded integer model.

\section{Key Code}
\label{app:code}

\paragraph{Intersection reservation.} \code{JointModel.path} in \code{optimizer.py} turns a selected
crossing into an optional interval of length $\tau^{X}_{kr} + \delta$ (\code{dt + self.gap}); absent visits
have zero entry and wait. One \code{NoOverlap} per resource then enforces the capacity of X and of each
bay.
\begin{lstlisting}
wait, entry = self.var('w_cross'), self.var('e_kr')
self.model.add(entry == at + wait).only_enforce_if(use)
self.model.add(entry == 0).only_enforce_if(~use)
self.model.add(wait == 0).only_enforce_if(~use)
self.resources['X'].append(self.model.new_optional_fixed_size_interval_var(
    entry, dt + self.gap, use, 'X'))
...
for intervals in self.resources.values():   # X, C1, C2
    m.add_no_overlap(intervals)
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Decoding and safe fallback.} A CP-SAT solution is replayed through the planner, validated,
given the terminal reserve when enabled, and compared with the Joint Greedy incumbent on the actual
objective; otherwise the incumbent is kept and
the reason recorded.
\begin{lstlisting}
decoded = joint.decode(solver)             # replay through Planner
build_result(decoded, base.results + tasks, 'cpsat',
             started, opts['norm'])        # independent validation
if opts['terminal_reserve']:               # robots active in this solve
    for k in active:                       # (index set abridged)
        decoded.ensure_reserve(k)          # charger reachable at end
if (selected is None or score(decoded, opts['norm'])
        <= score(selected, opts['norm']) + 1e-7):
    selected = decoded
else:
    metadata['fallback'] = 'joint hint has a lower decoded objective'
\end{lstlisting}
